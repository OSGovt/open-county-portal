**Short answer:** Open source covers the website well and the records piece partly. It does not realistically cover property tax or court payments. For those, the open-source part is only the front end. You still need a PCI-compliant payment processor and a connection to your tax or court system of record.

## Deadline
The DOJ pushed the ADA Title II web deadlines back a year. Large entities now must comply by April 26, 2027, and smaller entities and special districts by April 26, 2028. The size cutoff is 50,000 population. The technical standard has not changed: covered entities must still meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA. Third-party vendors are still in scope (per UsableNet's summary). That means a payment page you link to has to meet the standard too, so ask any vendor for an accessibility conformance report (VPAT/ACR).

## 1. Online payments (property tax and court costs)
I found no maintained open-source property tax billing or court payment system. Every example I found was commercial or run by a state judiciary.

- **Property tax software on the market:** Tyler Technologies, OpenGov and Harris Govern are the main ones. Edmunds GovTech and CentralSquare also sell it. Their portals also handle payments, so the payment piece comes bundled with them.
- **Court case management software:** JusticeWare lists a public portal with case search and online payments. I did not evaluate its accessibility or fit.
- **Statewide court payment portals:** Several states run one for all counties, including Wisconsin, Pennsylvania and South Dakota. If your state has one, joining it is likely far cheaper than building anything.
- **Self-hosting payments:** I'd advise against it. None of the open-source gateways reviewed were ready-to-use, PCI-compliant gateways out of the box. The hosted payment infrastructure itself requires commercial providers because of PCI compliance. If your site only redirects to or embeds a provider's hosted page, you can typically self-certify under SAQ A, the simplest PCI self-assessment type. If card data touches your servers, the burden grows sharply (SAQ D, roughly 330 requirements versus about 13). One person can't realistically carry that.
- **Open-source orchestration:** Hyperswitch is an open-source payments orchestrator that connects to multiple processors through one API. It still sits on top of a processor and doesn't replace one. I'd skip it at your scale.

## 2. Records search
"Records search" could mean two things: a public records request portal, or a searchable index of records such as land or court records. I only researched the first. Tell me if you meant the second.

- **NYC OpenRecords** ([GitHub](https://github.com/CityOfNewYork/NYCOpenRecords)): It is Apache-2.0 licensed. Requesters submit FOIL requests, and government employees manage, respond to and fulfill them. The stack is heavy: Python, PostgreSQL, ElasticSearch and Redis. The README lists old versions (RHEL 6/7), though the releases page shows v3.4.40. It is built around New York's FOIL law, so expect to customize it for your state.
- **RecordTrac** ([Code for America repo](https://github.com/codeforamerica/recordtrac)): It is open source, but looks abandoned. An 18F review noted no active development, and one fork says it is no longer actively maintained. I wouldn't deploy it on a public-facing system.
- **ArkCase** ([Armedia FOIA page](https://armedia.com/solution/foia/)): Armedia built a FOIA solution on the ArkCase open-source case management platform. It is vendor-supported, so you would likely end up with a services contract anyway.
- **Commercial comparison:** CivicPlus NextRequest is the usual SaaS choice.

## 3. Website at WCAG 2.1 AA
This is where open source works best.

- **Drupal:** It is open source with a long-term support release cycle. Its accessibility team tracks issues against WCAG success criteria (tagging issues by criterion). Drupal.org also publishes accessibility conformance reports ([ACR process](https://www.drupal.org/docs/getting-started/accessibility/accessibility-conformance-report-acr-process)).
- **LocalGov Drupal** ([site](https://localgovdrupal.org/products/localgov-drupal-cms)): This is a pre-configured Drupal for local councils. Its content and functionality are designed to meet or exceed WCAG 2.1 AA. It was built for UK councils, so it is not tuned to US counties, and I haven't seen evidence of US county adoption. It is still worth reviewing as a base.
- **Provus Gov** (an open-source Drupal solution, from [Promet Source](https://www.prometsource.com/blog/government-cms-comparison-guide)): This is a vendor-backed Drupal distribution. The accessibility claims come from the vendor's own comparison guide.
- **Caveat:** A CMS only gets you part of the way. Custom modules, editorial decisions and third-party embeds can all introduce violations. PDFs are a big risk. A PDF application form still in use has to be fixed. Plan for manual testing and editor training, not just a scanner.

## What I'd install
1. **Website:** Self-host **Drupal** with an accessible base theme, using LocalGov Drupal as a reference or starting point. Add Drupal's Webform module for forms; I haven't researched that module here. If you can't patch and monitor a CMS alone, buy managed Drupal hosting and keep the code open source. That's still no license lock-in.
2. **Payments:** Don't self-host. Ask whichever vendor holds your tax or court data whether they offer a hosted payment page. Check your state judiciary for a statewide court portal. Require an ACR for any payment page. Link to it from the Drupal site.
3. **Records:** Start with a Drupal Webform plus a shared inbox if volume is low. If volume is high, evaluate NYC OpenRecords against your state's public records law, or use NextRequest as SaaS.

**My one pick: Drupal for the website.** It is the only one of the three needs where self-hosting open source is mature, well-supported and plausible for one person. For payments, an open-source option would add PCI risk for no benefit. I'd put the vendor budget there and spend it on one narrowly scoped, accessible, hosted payment contract.

Two questions would sharpen this. What is your county's population, and so which deadline applies? And which systems hold your tax and court data today?
